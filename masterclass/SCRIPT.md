## OPEN — The Hook (0:00–2:18)

Hi. I'm Josée. I'm 58 years old, and I don't code.

Here's what I'm going to show you right now: how to build an AI digital business by doing something most people never try — replacing yourself. Not disappearing. Replacing the *tasks*. I built a team of AI employees that took over the writing, the designing, the scheduling, the parts of my business that used to eat my whole day. And what I got back wasn't just time. It was freedom.

Let me be specific about what that actually means, because "freedom" gets thrown around a lot and I don't want to be one more person saying a word that doesn't mean anything.

It means I get back 30-plus hours every single week. That's not a guess — that's the actual math of what disappeared off my plate once my employees took over. Thirty hours is most people's part-time job. Thirty hours is four full days. That's what I used to spend writing captions, designing graphics, scheduling posts, checking my work, chasing the next idea. Now an AI employee does each of those jobs, every day, without me sitting there.

And freedom means this: I decide what I want my day to look like, instead of my business deciding it for me. I press publish, then I close the laptop. I'm not chained to a phone. I'm not up at midnight fixing something. I built a business that runs, whether I'm sitting at my desk or out living my life.

Here's what this video is not. It is not a pitch for a course. It is not a promise about how much money you'll make — I'm not going to give you a number, because your results depend on you, not me. What I am going to give you is the actual system. Word for word, step by step, the same one running my business right now, today, while you watch this.

Here's the shape of this training: first, I'll tell you exactly why I had to change — because you should know the cost of doing it the old way before I show you the new one. Then I'll open the hood on the brain file, the one document that makes AI sound like you instead of a robot. Then I'll introduce you to my seven employees, one at a time, so you know exactly what each one does. Then I'll show you my actual daily routine — what a real morning looks like now. And I'll close with the exact first step, so you're not just watching this, you're building something by the end of it.

If you've ever thought "I want an online business, but I'm not techy enough" — stay with me. That was me too. Let's start.

## PART 1 — Why I Had to Change (2:18–4:46)

Before any of this, I ran a digital marketing agency for seven years.

From the outside, it looked like success. I had big clients. People in the industry knew my name. If you'd looked at my business card, you'd have thought I had it made.

Here's what nobody saw. My phone buzzed at 11pm and I answered it, because that's what you do when a client is paying you. I worked 80-hour weeks and told myself it was temporary, that it would slow down once we hit the next milestone. It never slowed down. There was always another fire, another deadline, another thing only I could do — because I'd built the whole business around me being available for everything.

I want to paint you the actual picture, not the polished version. I remember sitting at my desk on a Sunday afternoon, sun outside, everyone else living their lives, and I was answering emails because I couldn't shut it off. That wasn't a bad week. That was most weeks.

I kept telling myself the tiredness was temporary. It wasn't. My body had been quietly keeping score the whole time, and eventually it stopped being quiet about it. I burned out completely — not "needed a vacation" tired, but the kind where your body simply stops. I collapsed, and I ended up in the ICU.

I want you to really sit in that moment with me, because it's the entire reason this masterclass exists. I was lying in a hospital bed, hooked up to machines, and I asked myself one question: what is the point of building a business if it breaks you?

I didn't have an answer. Not a good one, not a bad one — nothing. Silence. So I made a decision right there: I shut the whole thing down.

That could have been the end of my story. For a while, honestly, it felt like it might be. I'd spent seven years building something, and I walked away from it with nothing to show for the effort except exhaustion.

But here's what I decided, once I could think straight again: if I ever built anything again, it would not run on my body being available every hour of every day. It would run on something else. It would be calm. It would be intentional. It would only need me for the parts that actually needed a human being — not for the parts a machine could do just as well, or better.

That's around the time AI got good enough to actually build with — not as a toy, but as a real working tool. So I sat down with Claude, by myself, no team, no developer, and I started teaching it who I was.

That one decision — sitting down and treating Claude like an employee I was training, not a search bar I was typing into — is the seed that grew into everything I'm about to show you.

## PART 2 — The Brain File (4:46–~7:40, recorded together with Part 3 in file 3)

The very first thing I built wasn't an employee. It was a foundation for every employee that came after: the brain file.

Think of a brain file as one document that holds everything an employee needs to know about you before it does a single piece of work. Your story. Your people — who you're actually talking to. And the hardest one to put into words: how you actually sound when you talk, not how a business "should" sound.

Here's why I'm spending real time on this instead of rushing to the fun part. If you skip the brain file and go straight to asking AI to write your content, here's what happens: you get content that's technically correct and completely lifeless. It reads like every other AI-written post on the internet — a little too polished, a little too generic, missing the thing that makes your actual voice yours. I call it robot content, and once you've seen it, you can't unsee it.

Building my brain file took about 20 minutes, sitting at my kitchen table with my laptop, answering questions the way I'd answer a new friend over coffee, not the way I'd fill out a corporate form. Here's roughly what I answered, and I'd encourage you to actually write your own answers down as I go through this, not just listen:

What's my real story — including the parts most people never see? For me, that meant actually writing down the agency years, the burnout, the ICU. Not the highlight reel version. The real one.

Who am I actually talking to? Not "everyone." A specific person, with a specific problem, at a specific moment in their life.

What words do I use, and — just as important — what words do I never use? I have phrases I don't say. Ever. That list matters as much as the list of words I do use.

What topics are completely off-limits for me? Every business has them. Mine does too.

And the hardest question: what do I sound like when I'm being completely honest, not performing for an audience?

I typed my answers straight into Claude, inside one project I built specifically for my business — think of it as the one filing cabinet everything else lives in. That brain file sits at the top of that project, and here's the part that makes the whole system work: every single employee I built after that reads from the exact same file. Not seven different versions of "who I am." One source of truth.

Here's the payoff, and it's the moment that convinced me this was real: once that brain file existed, I gave Claude an idea, and what came back sounded like me. Not perfect — I still read everything, I still adjust things — but recognizably, unmistakably me. Because it wasn't guessing at my voice. It was pulling from my actual words.

If you take away one single thing from this training, take this: brain file first. Every employee, every system, everything I'm about to show you is built on top of that one document. Skip it, and you're building a house with no foundation.

## PART 3 — Meet the Employees (~7:40–10:35, recorded together with Part 2 in file 3)

Now for the part most people ask me about first: the employees themselves.

Here's the mental shift that changed everything for me. I stopped thinking of Claude as a tool I poke at with random questions. I started thinking of it as a place where I hire employees — each one with exactly one job, doing that job the same reliable way every single time, in my voice, because it's reading from my brain file.

I have seven. Let me introduce you to each one, because I want you to understand exactly what they do and exactly where the line is.

**Employee one: the Post Writer.** This is the one that started it all. One job, described in one sentence: I give it an idea, it gives me back one finished post, in my voice, using my brain file. No hype, short sentences, one clear next step at the end. This employee alone gave me back hours every single day, because I went from staring at a blank page to reading a finished draft.

**Employee two: the Image Maker.** Every post needs a graphic, and this employee designs it — same colors, same style, every single time, because it's not guessing at my brand, it's reading it from the brain file.

**Employee three: the Reel Scripter.** Short-form video lives and dies on the hook. This employee turns one idea into a full script — the opening line, the beats, the caption — so I'm not sitting there trying to invent a hook from nothing.

**Employee four: the Viral Spotter.** This one watches what's actually resonating — patterns in what performs, mine and others' — and flags outliers worth paying attention to. It doesn't copy anyone's work. It notices what's working and brings me the pattern, not the content.

**Employee five: the Brand Reviewer.** I call this my quality gate. Before anything goes out the door, this employee checks it against my voice, my rules, my off-limits list from the brain file. If something's off-brand or off-tone, I hear about it before my audience ever does.

**Employee six: the Scheduler.** Once something's approved, this employee handles the part I used to dread most — sitting there at the right hour, on the right platform, pressing the button. Now it's queued and it goes out without me babysitting a calendar.

**Employee seven: the Video Editor.** This one assembles and polishes finished video — the cuts, the pacing, stitching the pieces into something finished.

Now here's the part I need you to hear clearly, because it's the difference between a system that serves you and one that runs away from you: these employees do the work. They do not make the decisions. I still decide what we create. I still approve everything before it goes anywhere. Nothing — nothing — goes out without me looking at it first. AI writes. It never publishes itself, and it never speaks for me without my say-so.

That boundary isn't a nice-to-have. It's the whole reason this system stays trustworthy. The moment you let a machine make the call instead of just doing the labor, you've built something very different from what I'm teaching you — and honestly, something I wouldn't want either.

## PART 4 — The 90/10 Routine (10:35–12:20)

So what does an actual morning look like, with seven employees quietly working?

I call it the 90/10 routine, and it's exactly what it sounds like: my employees handle 90% of the labor. I handle the 10% that genuinely needs a human being — and only that 10%.

Let me walk you through a real morning, not a theoretical one. I open my project. My Post Writer already has a draft sitting there, built from an idea I gave it the day before. I read it — takes me maybe a minute. If it sounds like me, I approve it on the spot. If a line feels off, I don't rewrite the whole thing — I just tell it what to fix, one sentence, and it hands back a corrected version. My Image Maker already has the matching graphic ready. My Brand Reviewer has flagged anything that needs a second look before I even get to it. My Scheduler already has the finished piece queued for the right time on the right platform.

My entire job, most mornings, comes down to three verbs: decide, approve, publish. That's it. That's the job description for running a content business now.

And here's what I never automated, on purpose, and never will: when someone in my community asks me a real question, the answer comes from me. Not an employee. Me. AI writes my content — it does not answer for me. I kept that line human on purpose, because that's the part people can actually feel the difference on, and it's the one piece of this business I'd never hand off to anything, no matter how good the technology gets.

That's the whole shape of my day now. Hold that up against the 80-hour weeks and the midnight phone calls I told you about earlier. Same person. Same drive to build something. Completely different business.

## PART 5 — Where You Start (12:20–13:41)

I know exactly what some of you are thinking right now: "Josée, that's seven employees. I don't even know how to open Claude."

Good. Because you're not building seven today. You're building one.

Here is the exact first employee to hire, right now, even if you have never opened Claude in your life:

Open Claude. Start one project — just one, dedicated only to your business. This becomes your filing cabinet. Inside it, type this out, exactly, the same way you'd text a friend:

"You are my Post Writer. Your only job: I give you one idea, you give me one finished post in my voice. Short sentences. Plain words. No hype. One clear next step at the end. Ask me for today's idea."

Read that back. There is no code in that sentence. There's no technical skill required. If you can type a text message to a friend, you can type that into Claude. That's the entire bar for entry.

Give it one real idea today — something you'd actually consider posting. Read what comes back. If it doesn't sound quite like you yet, that's completely normal, and it's exactly what the brain file step fixes — which is covered start to finish in the free guide waiting for you when you join my community.

One employee. Get it running smoothly. Then, and only then, add the next one. That's how a foundation becomes a full team — the same way mine did.
